Universal basic income in a society of LLM agents

A replication of Section 7.4 of AgentSociety (Piao et al., 2025, arXiv:2502.08691), the universal basic income experiment, with 50 Texas residents played by Claude Sonnet 5.5 in a closed economy. Two economies run for 36 months; in one, every citizen receives an unconditional $1,000 a month from month 12. The paper reports that the payment raised consumption and lowered depression (CES-D). This page shows what the replication found, and lets you step through every month and read each citizen's decision. An independent review (below) found that most of the differences between the arms come from the simulation's mechanics, not from the citizens' choices; read the results with it.

Results

Means over the 24 months with the payment (months 12–35), UBI arm against the control arm. The two arms share months 0–11 exactly (the UBI arm starts from the control arm's state at month 12); they differ only in the payment and in what the model does with it.

How to read these numbers. An adversarial review (Claude Opus 5.5, 2026-10-26) rebuilt both arms exactly from the recorded choices and found:

  • The citizens' choices did not respond to the UBI. 43 of 50 gave the same work and spending answer in both arms for all 24 months; most chose work 0.80 and spend 0.05 whatever their wage. Running the UBI economy with the control arm's choices reproduces the spending effect almost exactly.
  • The extra spending (0.41 per UBI dollar) is accounting, not behaviour: citizens spend a fixed share of a balance the UBI enlarges, so the ratio climbs as the payments pile up (0.27 in year 1, 0.54 in year 2, about 0.9 after ten years). Its closeness to the OpenResearch figures is a coincidence of timing.
  • Output could not change: the firm hires every hour offered in every month, so output equals the hours citizens offered, and they offered the same.
  • The inflation in both arms is an artefact: the output gap counts investment orders the firm never fills. Measured on goods actually delivered there is no excess demand. Inflation reaches 7.9% (control) and 9.5% (UBI) in year 3, and the Taylor rule as specified (0.5 per point of inflation) cannot rein it in.
  • "Crowding out" is a queue: consumers are served first and investment gets the rest; the interest rate plays no part.
  • The savings growth is not sustainable: citizens save about half their income, investment goods are taken without payment, and household deposits are the firm's accumulated losses. Without the UBI the firm would hit its credit limit around month 76 and output would collapse. The +15% savings in the UBI arm is mechanical and the opposite of what the OpenResearch study found.
  • Depression cannot be tested here: CES-D scores (about 5 of 60) do not respond to wealth or wages; the +0.2 difference is noise (standard error 0.15).

What holds: the accounting (money and goods balance every month), the clean branch at month 12, no sign that the model recognised the study, and every reply parsed. The replication is therefore best read as a demonstration that this setup produces paper-like headline effects mechanically: the original's decision rule (spend a share of the balance) and its JSON-only prompt, inside the closed economy built here (whose investment and price rules cause the inflation). It is not evidence about how people, or LLM agents, respond to a basic income.

Method

What was replicated

The paper's text gives §7.4 only in outline; its protocol is in the engine's v1 code (MonthEconomyPlanBlock and the firm, bank, government and statistics agents). Each month every citizen gets one fixed prompt (below) and answers two numbers: a propensity to work (× 168 hours offered) and the share of their savings and income to spend on goods. Every third month they fill in the 20-item CES-D; in the UBI arm, at months 12 and 24, they give an opinion. The prompt is the original's, word for word, with one added sentence of persona.

The economy

The original's economy was not closed: wages were created from nothing and spending and taxes vanished, so prices and wages fell while real GDP rose. This replication closes it, in steps the reviewers checked:

  • One firm makes the essential goods, at one unit per hour worked. It pays wages from its cash and bank credit and receives all spending; it hires only the hours it needs to cover demand plus a month of stock.
  • Prices are sticky (Mankiw's short-run aggregate supply): p = E[p] + ((1−s)φ/s)·y, with 10% of prices resetting each month (s = 0.9), φ = 0.15, expected inflation anchored at 2% and y the output gap. Wages move with the price level.
  • Savings come back as investment, following Chawwa & Rachmanto's closed-economy model: the firm buys investment goods i = ī·e−(r−r₀), financed by bank credit, with ī set in month 0 to the citizens' planned saving. Without it, the citizens' saving (about half of income) left demand short and the firm hired only half the hours offered.
  • The government withholds 2020 federal bracket taxes, pays interest on savings at the policy rate, and returns everything else equally, as the prompt tells citizens it does. The central bank follows a Taylor rule with a −0.5% floor.
  • The UBI comes from outside the economy, like the philanthropic funds of the OpenResearch study: it is the one inflow of money. Total money is checked every month.

Who the citizens are

50 people from NVIDIA's Nemotron-Personas-USA (CC BY 4.0), Texas residents aged 18–64 drawn in proportion to the state's sex × age-decade distribution, from occupations that make sense in an economy of only them: jobs that grow, make, build, move, sell or repair the goods, or care for the people who do (3 places reserved for farming and ranching). Hourly wages are the BLS OEWS May 2023 Texas median for each occupation ($13.00–$103.65, median $20.41). Everyone starts with $30,000 in savings, as in the original code.

What the model received

Citizens are Claude Sonnet 5.5 at low reasoning effort, called through claude -p from an empty directory with no tools, no settings and a one-line neutral system prompt; the host adds a line naming the Claude Agent SDK, a reminder with the account's email, and an environment block (platform, model, date). Where this departs from the paper, and why


    

  1. UBI from month 12 for 24 months, not from month 96; the comparison window keeps the paper's 24 months.
    2. 

  2. A closed economy with investment (above), instead of the original's open money flows.
    3. 

  3. The UBI arm starts from the control arm's exact state at month 12. The paper ran two separate simulations; here any gap after month 12 is the payment plus sampling noise.
    4. 

  4. Only the monthly economic loop is simulated: the original's daily life (movement, social contact) enters none of the §7.4 measures.
    5. 

  5. Bank units corrected and a −0.5% floor: the released Taylor rule mixed percent and fractions and credited the annual rate every month.
    6. 

  6. Taxes actually collected: the released government agent never reached its tax routine.
    7. 

  7. CES-D scored as published (items 4, 8, 12, 16 reversed); the released code's reversal never fired. Its literal score is stored too.
    8. 

  8. Citizens and wages from data (Nemotron personas, BLS wages), instead of the original's random wages.

Limits


    

  • One model, one seed, two arms, 50 citizens: no confidence intervals.
    • 

  • claude -p returns no reasoning text and no log-probabilities; both facts are recorded on every call.
    • 

  • Investment barely responds to the interest rate (the paper's elasticity, and the Taylor rule first moves at month 23), so it works as a steady channel for saving rather than a policy lever. Investment goods do not add capacity, and the firm's bank debt grows with the citizens' deposits.
    • 

  • Demand exceeds what full-time work can make from month 1: citizens are served first and the firm's investment gets what is left, so investment shrinks over the run and its unmet part keeps the output gap positive (about +0.3). That unmet demand drives all of the inflation above the 2% anchor (6–8% a year in the control arm, up to 9.5% in the UBI arm). The Taylor rule's 0.5 response to inflation is too weak to stop it.
    • 

  • The citizens' replies are bare JSON, as the original prompt demands ("Any other output words are NOT allowed"), and the model rarely moved from its first answer; with no reasoning text or log-probabilities from the host, why it did not respond cannot be examined.
    • 

  • The model may know the policy debate; nothing in the prompt names the study.
    • 



The real-world comparison the paper makes


Bartik et al. (2024, NBER w32784; the OpenResearch unconditional cash study, $1,000 a month for three years to 1,000 low-income adults in Texas and Illinois, against $50 a month for 2,000 controls) found spending up by at least $300 a month, with marginal propensities to consume of roughly 0.4–0.7, and no lasting change in net worth. The companion health paper (Miller et al., 2024, NBER w32711) found that early gains in stress and mental health did not last beyond the first year. In that study the transfer went to a few households in a large economy, so it could not move prices or wages; here it goes to everyone.
