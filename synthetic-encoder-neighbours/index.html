What a skill-trained text encoder changes: items near in one embedding space and far in the other

The items are 4,946 multiple-choice questions about synthetic Blender videos (900 scenes, each written to target one skill node of a skill tree; answers read from the scene file). How they are made, with sample clips: Synthetic video QA from Blender scenes. Here they are embedded twice, by a general sentence embedder and by the same embedder after training on skill-tree labels, to show which items each one treats as similar.

Clips play the 12-second, 2 fps video the questions are about; the encoders themselves see only the text (question + options). Correct answers are highlighted when "Show answers" is on.

The two spaces

To cluster these items by skill, each question is embedded as text (question + options; the encoder never sees the video). Two spaces are compared:

  • Base: gte-modernbert-base, a general sentence embedder, frozen.
  • Trained encoder: the same model fine-tuned on about 50k video-benchmark questions, each labelled by an LLM with its path in the skill tree, so that questions sharing skills land close together. None of the synthetic items on this page were in its training data.

Nearness below is a rank: for item i, rank 1 is its nearest other item by cosine similarity among all items. Items from the same scene are excluded, because they share a scene description and would be near for a trivial reason.

Each scene's "skill node" is the one its brief targeted. Questions inside a scene often exercise neighbouring skills too, so this is a rough yardstick, not ground truth.

What the examples suggest (a reading, not a measurement)

  • The base model groups by shared nouns and setting: "the coffee table", "the walker", "the camera", "the sign". Two questions about a coffee table are near even when one asks how the camera orbits it and the other how many objects rest on it.
  • The trained encoder groups by what has to be judged: how far things travel relative to each other, which side something ends up on, whether something stripes or changes. Different objects and wording do not keep them apart.
  • It also leans on the answer format: "which side…" questions (left/right of an object, which side is lit) can sit together even when the skills differ, and for some items the base model's neighbours match the brief's skill node better (the walker / egocentric-turn anchor). The trained encoder's neighbours share the brief's skill node more often overall (table above), not every time.
