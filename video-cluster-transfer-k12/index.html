Where does narrow fine-tuning help? K = 12 cluster transfer on two video LLMs

63,610 video multiple-choice questions from 27 benchmarks were split into 12 clusters by k-means, in three ways: in the skill-encoder embedding space (a sentence embedder fine-tuned on LLM skill labels), in the frozen embedding space it started from (gte-modernbert-base), and at random with the same cluster sizes (the control). For each cluster, a LoRA adapter was trained on 544 of its questions (2 seeds) and every adapter was scored on the held-out questions of all 12 clusters (7,399 items, split by source video), with the video and blind (question only). Models: Qwen3.5-9B and Gemma-4-12B-it, after 1 pass (34 steps) or 4 passes (136 steps; passes 2–4 use reworded questions); each figure has a Training toggle between the two.

All numbers are accuracy changes in percentage points against the same model before fine-tuning, averaged over the two seeds. Data: data.json. Cluster names were written by a language model from sample questions and checked by a blind sorting test (78 % / 77 % of held-out items placed in the right cluster); the random partition has no names.

1–2. Trained-on × scored-on matrices

Columns are the cluster an adapter was trained on; rows are the cluster it was scored on; the boxed diagonal is the adapter's own cluster. Row-centred subtracts, from each row, the mean change that the other clusters' adapters produce on it, so a uniformly blue or red row (a cluster every adapter helps or hurts) turns neutral and the diagonal shows own-cluster gain beyond that. Benchmark restricts the scored items to one benchmark (its rows only; cells with fewer than 20 test items are blank), which asks whether "own cluster" just means "own benchmark".

Panel subtitles are unweighted means over the shown cells (the item-weighted within-benchmark contrast is in BUILD_LOG 2026-11-24). Colour is capped at ±15 points; hover a cell for its exact value and item count.

3. Does cluster-pair similarity predict cross-cluster transfer?

Each dot is one off-diagonal cell (adapter trained on cluster i, scored on cluster j; 132 per panel). The x-axis is how similar the adapter's training questions are to cluster j's test questions: the cosine between their mean embeddings (centroid) or the mean, over test questions, of the cosine to the nearest training question (nearest neighbour), in either embedding space. With cluster effects removed (the primary analysis), both axes are residuals after removing a trained-on and a scored-on cluster effect, so the question is: for a given scored-on cluster, does the closer adapter help more, beyond that adapter helping everywhere?

r = Pearson correlation over the 132 cells shown. Each partition was fitted in one space, so its clusters are compact in that space; compare the two partitions. The random partition has no cluster structure: its r is a noise reference.

4. Item-level: gain by distance to the training set

For every adapter and every scored item, the item's distance to the adapter's training questions is measured in two ways. 10 nearest (the primary measure): the mean cosine distance to the 10 closest training questions. Training centroid: the cosine distance to the mean embedding of all the adapter's training questions, i.e. how far the item is from the training set's average question. Items are split into fifths of distance within each adapter × benchmark (so benchmark identity cannot carry the effect); the line is the mean accuracy change in each fifth, closest to farthest, with ±1 SE clustered by source video. Items whose exact question text was in the adapter's training set are left out.

Fifths: within benchmark (the primary view) cuts fifths separately inside each benchmark, so every fifth holds the same benchmark mix and only distance within a benchmark can matter. All benchmarks pooled cuts fifths over all of an adapter's scored items at once; then the closest fifth is mostly the adapter's own benchmarks, and the curve mixes the benchmark effect with the distance effect. The gap between the two views shows how much of the distance effect is benchmark identity.

The top row measures distance in the trained (skill-encoder) space, the bottom row in the baseline (frozen-embedding) space. Trained on shows a single adapter (one trained-on cluster, both seeds) instead of the pooled adapters; pick a partition under Adapters first. Single adapters are noisier: check the error bars.

Random-partition training sets are mixtures of every kind of question, so their centroid sits near the middle of the item cloud and centroid distance barely varies across items; with those adapters, prefer the 10-nearest measure. The y-axis is shared across both distance measures, both fifth views, both training lengths and both spaces, so switching does not rescale it.

The skill encoder was trained on these items' skill labels, so its distances have seen the items; on benchmarks the encoder never saw, the item-level slopes were ≈ 0 in both spaces (earlier band-adapter run).

What stands out

  • The PhysBench cluster (g8 in the encoder partition) inflates the averages. Its own-cluster gain on Qwen is +24 (1 pass) and +38 (4 passes); without it Qwen's own − other gap is +4.5 / +6.1 instead of +6.0 / +8.6.
  • After 1 pass, most of Gemma's off-cluster loss is one adapter. The g8 adapter lowers every other cluster (mean −16, down to −27); without it Gemma's off-cluster change is −0.6, not −1.8. After 4 passes the loss is spread over almost every adapter.
  • Holding the benchmark fixed keeps most of the diagonal. Within CLEVRER, TemporalBench, PhysBench or Perception Test, the own-cluster cell is on average 2 to 23 points above the other adapters in its row (row-centred view), though it is not always the single best cell (Perception Test: own best in 1–3 of 7 rows). Benchmark identity does not explain the locality; what remains confounded is that clusters within a benchmark also separate question templates.
  • Cluster-pair similarity barely predicts which other clusters an adapter helps (r ≤ 0.3 after cluster effects, and the larger values sit in different partitions on the two models), while item-level distance does: on Qwen the farthest fifth gains about half as much as the rest; on Gemma after 4 passes every fifth loses and the farthest loses most.
  • Without video, the extra passes add gains that cannot be visual. Switch the matrices to blind: the own-cluster diagonal grows with passes on both models.
